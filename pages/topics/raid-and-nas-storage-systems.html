05 · RAID and NAS

Different arrangements, different compromises

A RAID level names an arrangement of data across drives. The number is a label, not a score: a higher number is not automatically better.

0

Speed and space

Drives share the work; all installed space can hold files.

The cost: no protection if a drive fails.

Read all five previews

RAID 0

All capacity available. Trade-off: No redundancy.

RAID 1

Simple continued access. Trade-off: 50% capacity in a two-drive mirror.

RAID 5

Capacity with fault tolerance. Trade-off: Parity overhead; vulnerable during rebuild.

RAID 6

Protection against a second failure. Trade-off: Two drives’ worth of capacity overhead.

RAID 10

Performance and availability. Trade-off: 50% capacity; pair-dependent failures.

09 · RAID and NAS

Mirroring: what do we gain and give up?

Keep access after a failure

The surviving drive has a complete copy.

Reads can share the work

Different read requests can use either copy.

Every write goes to both

Usually similar to one drive, or slower with overhead; no equivalent doubling of write speed.

Pay for the extra copy

Two 2 TB drives hold 2 TB of different files.

Read gains depend on the workload and how the RAID system is implemented.

10 · RAID and NAS

Parity: recovery without copying everything

Parity is calculated recovery information. Keep it alongside the data so a missing piece can be worked out.

Copy every piece

ABCA copyB copyC copy

3 data blocks + 3 copied blocks

Calculate recovery information

ABCP

3 data blocks + 1 parity block of the same size

Less extra storage than a full second copy — but calculation and recovery take work.

This single parity block can help recover one missing block, using the other blocks. It cannot recover two missing blocks.

11 · RAID and NAS

A tiny rule: same or different?

A bit is a 0 or a 1. Compare two bits: same → 0, different → 1.

00→0Same
11→0Same
01→1Different
10→1Different

This comparison is called XOR (exclusive OR). We can use its result as a parity bit. No arithmetic carry or addition is involved.

Different → parity 1

A stripe is one row of blocks across the drives. The gold parity block protects the data in its own row.

P-A protects A1 and A2; P-B protects B1 and B2; P-C protects C1 and C2.

Parity changes position. There is no dedicated “parity drive”.

RAID 0: when would it fit?

✓ A good fit

Video editing scratch space

25 · RAID and NAS

RAID 6: when would it fit?

✓ A good fit

A large museum image archive

Large drives may take a long time to rebuild. Surviving a second failure during that time matters more than maximum usable space.

✕ A poor fit

A small, write-heavy temporary workspace

Extra parity uses capacity and adds write work. That cost may bring little value if all temporary files can be recreated.

27 · RAID and NAS

RAID 10: when would it fit?

✓ A good fit

An online shop’s order database

Frequent small updates need good write performance and continued access after a drive failure. The business can afford the mirror copies.

✕ A poor fit

A requirement to survive any two failures

40 · RAID and NAS

Spot the misconception

Copies, parity and backups

“RAID is a backup.”

It has no automatic history of deleted or damaged files.

“Parity is a whole spare copy.”

It is calculated information used with surviving data.

“Striping protects against failure.”

Striping alone has no redundancy.

Failure limits

“RAID 5 survives two failures.”

Single parity supports one failed drive.

“RAID 6 only survives one.”

Two independent parity sets support any two failed drives.

“RAID 10 survives any two.”

Both drives in one mirror pair failing loses the array.

Choosing storage

“NAS and RAID are the same.”

NAS shares files; RAID organises drives.

“A NAS is just an external drive.”

It is a computer providing file services.

“More usable space always wins.”

Balance space against speed and continued access.

4 marksExplain

Question 1

Explain why RAID 1 provides greater fault tolerance than RAID 0.

Show answer guide
  • RAID 0 stripes blocks without redundancy. A drive failure loses required pieces.
  • A simple RAID 1 mirror stores the same data on both drives.
  • If either mirrored drive fails, the other still contains the files and can serve them.
  • Link the difference to reduced disruption, with the trade-off of half the raw capacity.
6 marksCompare

Question 2

Compare RAID 5 and RAID 6 for a business file server.

Show answer guide
  • Both stripe data and distribute parity rather than keeping full mirror copies.
  • RAID 5 needs at least 3 drives, tolerates 1 failure and gives (N − 1) × S usable.
  • RAID 6 needs at least 4 drives, tolerates 2 failures and gives (N − 2) × S usable.
  • With four 2 TB drives: 6 TB for RAID 5 versus 4 TB for RAID 6.
  • RAID 6 provides protection against a second failure during a rebuild, at the cost of extra capacity and parity overhead.
  • Relate the balance to the file server’s workload, capacity needs and downtime risk.
8 marksAnalyse

Question 3

A company has a four-bay NAS with four 2 TB drives, used by 40 employees. Analyse whether RAID 5 or RAID 10 would be more suitable.

Show answer guide
  • RAID 5 gives 6 TB; RAID 10 gives 4 TB. Explain how this affects room for shared files.
  • RAID 10 avoids parity calculation and can suit frequent small writes; RAID 5 can offer a useful capacity/read balance.
  • Both survive any one drive failure. RAID 10 may survive more, only if no mirror pair loses both members.
  • RAID 5 reconstructs from surviving data/parity; RAID 10 copies from a surviving mirror. Rebuild time depends on workload and drives; do not promise fixed times.
  • Both reduce disruption from drive failure, but neither protects against a whole NAS or network outage.
  • Discuss the higher cost per usable TB of RAID 10. User count alone does not tell us the workload.
  • Give a conditional conclusion: capacity-led file sharing may favour RAID 5; write-heavy work may justify RAID 10.
12 marksEvaluate

Question 4

A media company wants centralised storage for large project files. Many users need access, downtime must be minimised, and performance matters. Evaluate a suitable storage solution.

Show answer guide
  • A NAS provides shared folders and central permissions for file users. A SAN may suit server-hosted applications needing block storage, but is more complex and not automatically necessary.
  • Justify a RAID level: for example RAID 6 for protection during long rebuilds, or RAID 10 for write performance if its usable capacity is sufficient.
  • Explain the capacity trade-off using the drive examples; connect the remaining file space to growing project files. A formula is not needed.
  • Explain the supported drive failures and what happens during rebuild. Avoid claiming every arrangement survives any two failures.
  • Large transfers and concurrent users make network throughput important. More disks cannot overcome every network bottleneck.
  • RAID alone does not remove enclosure, network or security risks. Briefly acknowledge remaining sources of downtime.
  • Separate backups are still needed for deletion, ransomware or loss of the device. Do not treat parity as backup.
  • Weigh capacity, cost, speed, administration and availability. End with a justified recommendation and state what workload information could change it.

Glossary

Quick glossary

RAID
Redundant Array of Independent Disks: multiple drives organised as one storage system.
Raw capacity
The total physical capacity of the drives.
Usable capacity
Space available for files after redundancy overhead.
Striping
Spreading data blocks across drives.
Mirroring
Keeping duplicate copies of the same data.
Parity
Calculated information used with remaining data to reconstruct missing blocks.
Fault tolerance
Continuing to operate after a supported hardware failure.
Degraded
Operating with reduced redundancy after a failure.
Rebuild
Recreating missing drive contents on a replacement.
NAS
Network Attached Storage: a computer/appliance providing shared files over a network.
SAN
Storage Area Network: a specialised network commonly providing block storage to servers.

Remember

  • RAID organises drives.
  • NAS shares files over a network.
  • Fault tolerance has a failure limit.
  • RAID does not replace backup.
