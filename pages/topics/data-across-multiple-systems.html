01 · Data across multiple systems

Multiple computers. Different ways to organise data.

A business can use several computers without their records updating together. It must decide where the main records live and how changes reach other people.

The number of computers does not tell us how the data is organised. These are two starting arrangements; organisations can also combine approaches.

These computers may all have internet access and email. “Separate copies” describes how the records are maintained, not whether the machines have a network connection. Different files can also contain different task-specific fields.

03 · Data across multiple systems

Why might separate copies be enough?

When the business is small and changes are rare, a simple arrangement can meet its needs.

Existing tools

Low initial spending

Use the PCs and spreadsheets staff already have.

Local work

Keep working offline

A downloaded van schedule can be read without a connection.

Familiar routine

Little initial disruption

Each team keeps a layout and process it understands.

This is more manageable with few orders, few changes and a clear person responsible for passing updates on.

More explanation and examples

Local copies can remain usable if another team’s computer or a shared connection fails. However, staff may be working from old information. Each copy still needs appropriate access control and backup, and staff time spent coordinating changes is a cost.

The upgrade: one maintained order record

The business replaces its separate order files with a shared order system. Sales checks the customer’s request and updates the delivery slot once.

Centralised data has a common main store. Several computers still use it; staff no longer maintain three independent versions of this order.

More explanation and examples

The service could run on company servers or be hosted elsewhere. One common source does not mean one physical server: it can use several machines and managed replicas. Clients may need to refresh to retrieve a change. Some specialist applications instead keep their own copies and exchange updates; we will examine that under productivity.

Sharing a source improves consistency, not guaranteed accuracy. If the checked delivery slot is entered incorrectly, every team could use the same wrong date.

The driver sees contact details for assigned deliveries. Needing a customer’s number does not justify access to every customer or to payroll.

More explanation and examples

Permissions can restrict both the fields and the records a user can access. Editing one field does not imply permission to delete a record. Payroll is managed by separately authorised staff. Limiting access supports security, while granting enough access lets people do their jobs.

10 · Cost

Cost: compare the whole cost over time

A shared order platform brings costs beyond the software price. Existing spreadsheets also use staff time and resources.

  • Train staff; cover their time
During use

  • Hosting, devices and connectivity
  • Support, security and backups

Few changesThe existing process may be affordable.
Frequent changesRepeated errors may justify investment.

More explanation and examples

Compare total setup and running costs over a realistic period against expected benefits. A free licence does not make migration, support or hosting free. Staff time saved can be used for other work without lowering wages; do not count it automatically as cash saved.

11 · Implementation

Implementation: make the change usable

Implementation is the whole move into successful everyday use. Installing software is only one part.

Prepare

1 · Plan & move

  • Agree fields, roles and responsibilities.
  • Resolve conflicting copies.
  • Migrate records and connect required systems.

Prove

2 · Test & train

  • Follow an order change through to dispatch.
  • Check records and permissions.
  • Let staff practise real tasks.

Launch

3 · Change over & support

  • Pilot with a small team.
  • Choose a suitable launch time and fallback.
  • Help users and correct problems.

A rushed launch can delay deliveries even if the software works. Plan time for data checks and staff learning.

More explanation and examples

Keep a protected copy of original data and an agreed fallback before changeover. Compatibility with specialist software, field formats and record identifiers needs testing. A pilot or phased rollout can reveal problems before all teams depend on the system.

16 · Security

Security: another device is another route in

Oak & Room’s delivery tablet opens shared orders. The driver leaves it unlocked in the van.

Follow the exposure

  1. Open sessionSomeone uses the driver's signed-in tablet.
  2. Customer detailsThey can read names, phone numbers and delivery addresses.
  3. People affectedCustomers lose privacy; the company must deal with the incident.

Reduce the exposure

Lock the screen; limit the accountRequire an unlock check and show only the deliveries the driver needs.

Wider access creates more routes to protect. Separate files also need protection against misuse, damage and loss.

More explanation and examples

The tablet does not need a copy of every customer record to expose data: a signed-in session can request records the account is allowed to see. Screen locking reduces casual access through an unattended device. Permissions limit how much information that session can reach.

Separate local files may be copied, stolen, altered or lost too. A shared system can make permissions and security updates more consistent, but an account with excessive permissions could expose many records. Security depends on how either arrangement is managed.

17 · Security

Security: control what each account can do

Authentication

Who are you?

The driver signs in with a personal account. Multi-factor authentication adds a check beyond the password.

→

Permissions

What may you do?

View assigned delivery addresses and update delivery status. Staff payroll remains blocked.

Activity logReview unusual requests and changes
10:12Driver accountRequested payrollPermission blocked it

Permissions block unauthorised actions. Logs record activity so someone can investigate and respond.

More explanation and examples

Authentication checks identity; authorisation determines what that identity may view, change or delete. Signing in successfully does not grant access to every record. Give each person the access required for their job and review it when their duties change.

Multi-factor authentication can protect an account if its password is stolen, but it does not make an already unlocked session safe. Shared permissions can be easier to manage consistently than separate rules on many files.

Logs can record successful access, changes and blocked requests. They support monitoring and investigation; they do not prevent every misuse. An unusual event needs review, and a confirmed problem needs a response such as restricting an account or correcting affected records.

While data travels

Secure the devices

Delivery tablet + order server

✓ Security updates✓ Approved software✓ Protected access

Reduce opportunities for malware and unauthorised users.

More explanation and examples

Protect the shared service as well as the devices that use it. Updates reduce known vulnerabilities; approved software and device controls reduce opportunities for malware. Automatic screen locking helps protect unattended tablets.

Confidentiality is only part of security. Unauthorised changes could send furniture to the wrong address, and deleted orders could stop deliveries. Appropriate edit/delete permissions, checked backups and recovery procedures help protect against these effects.

19 · Security

Security: match protection to potential harm

Apply the same idea to a college: the consequences depend on the information involved.

Public

Course timetable

Personal

Contact details

Only relevant staff need them.

If exposedA student's home address or phone number becomes known.
Highly sensitive

Safeguarding record

Requires specially authorised access.

If exposed, altered or lostA student's safety or support may be put at risk.

Protect against unauthorised access, alteration and loss. Public data still needs to stay accurate and available.

More explanation and examples

A public timetable needs protection against unauthorised changes even though reading it is allowed. Incorrect contact details can stop an important message reaching home. Missing or altered safeguarding information may prevent appropriate support.

The greater the potential harm, the more carefully access and protection must be designed. A staff job title alone does not justify access to every sensitive record. Different roles can use the same shared platform while seeing different information.

Exam technique: build a balanced answer

For a question asking you to evaluate a change, develop benefits and concerns, then decide which matter most in this scenario.

Example chain · the college introduces shared records

  1. 1 · Benefit

    Reception can update a student’s course once…

  2. 2 · Impact

    …so teachers can use current class lists without requesting another copy…

  3. 3 · Application

    …helping Alex, a student who has changed course, appear on the correct register and reducing staff checking.

4 · Trade-off, with its own explained impact

However, a shared-service outage could stop several teachers opening registers at once, delaying attendance checks across the college.

Apply both sides to the scenario. Develop more relevant chains as the question requires; this short example is only part of an extended answer.

More explanation and examples

This is a flexible planning aid, not a required Pearson structure. For a short “explain” question, develop the requested point and its effect. For a longer analysis, connect causes and consequences. When evaluation or a recommendation is required, weigh the important effects and support your final decision.

Pearson’s Computing Unit 2 sample assessment materials, printed pp. 29–31, show that requirements vary with the question: the 12-mark evaluation includes a justified conclusion; the 8-mark explanation uses different criteria. This lesson’s wording and example are our own.

24 · Data across multiple systems

Finish by weighing the effects

Why the benefit matters

Correct registers are needed every lesson. Removing repeated checking can help staff every day.

Why the concern matters

An outage can interrupt several classes at once. That makes reliable access and a safe fallback important.

5 · Justified judgement

On balance, shared records are likely to help this college because accurate class lists and fewer repeated updates benefit daily teaching. These gains are likely to outweigh the setup effort if migration is tested, staff are trained and essential attendance work has a safe fallback.

If access remains unreliable, delaying the full rollout would be more sensible.

Decision + most important reason + conditions. Explain why those conditions matter; adding the word “if” alone is not evaluation.

An extended answer needs enough developed, relevant reasoning for the question. Five labels, five definitions or five sentences do not guarantee high marks.

27 · Data across multiple systems

Exam-style practice

Explain consequences for staff, students and the college. These are practice questions, not an official mark scheme.

Draft answers save automatically in this browser.

4 marks

Question 1

Explain two benefits of allowing authorised staff to access shared data from multiple systems.

Show answer guide
  • Explain one access benefit with its effect: teachers can use current attendance in classrooms, allowing quicker follow-up.
  • Explain a second distinct benefit: reception updates contacts once, reducing repeated entry and mistakes across departments.
  • For four marks, develop two benefit → applied impact links, rather than naming access and productivity.
4 marks

Question 2

Explain two implementation issues an organisation may face when introducing a shared information system.

Show answer guide
  • Migration may place data in the wrong field or omit records; this could put a student on the wrong register, so source comparisons and workflow tests are needed.
  • Staff may not know the new process, slowing attendance entry; training and launch support reduce disruption.
  • For four marks, explain two distinct issues and their consequences. Other applied issues, such as integration failures or badly timed changeover, can also be valid.
6 marks

Question 3

Discuss how using data across multiple systems could affect productivity in a college.

Show answer guide
  • Current contacts available across authorised systems reduce duplicate typing and speed up communication with families.
  • Reliable shared data can speed up reporting and leave more staff time for student support.
  • Poor integration, downtime or inadequate training can instead create checking, correction and delays across several departments.
  • Develop both benefits and concerns with college examples. Reach a supported judgement that depends on reliability, implementation and user support.
8 marks

Question 4

Analyse the security and access implications of allowing staff to use student data from multiple devices and locations.

Show answer guide
  • Remote and classroom access let authorised staff respond sooner, but access should match the task: attendance access does not justify safeguarding access.
  • More devices and connections create more possible routes for misuse. Link authentication, permissions and protected devices to limiting that exposure.
  • Consistent permissions and logs may improve oversight; a compromised account with excessive access could affect many records.
  • Explain harm to individuals: exposed contact or safeguarding information can affect privacy or safety; altered records can misdirect support.
  • Connect wider access to the protection it needs. Weigh convenience against sensitivity and justify appropriate restrictions rather than simply banning or allowing all remote access.
12 marks

Question 5

A college plans to replace several separate systems with one connected student-information platform. Evaluate the likely impact on the organisation.

Show answer guide
  • Access and productivity: current course/contact records could reduce duplicate entry and speed up registers, enquiries and reports. Explain the effect on staff and students.
  • Cost: compare migration, training and setup with continuing licences/support and possible savings in staff time. Benefits may take time to repay initial costs.
  • Implementation: migrating Alex’s records incorrectly or launching during registration could disrupt several teams. Testing, training and a phased changeover need time and resources.
  • Security: consistent permissions may improve control, but wider access and sensitive records require protection. Explain how limiting access affects convenience and risk.
  • Connect factors: paying for training may temporarily reduce productivity but prevent recurring errors; funding protection can make remote access more appropriate.
  • For higher marks, weigh the important impacts and reach a conditional recommendation grounded in the college’s needs. Explain what would change the judgement, such as unreliable integration or unaffordable support. A five-factor list alone is insufficient.

Glossary

Quick glossary

Separate copies
Independently maintained versions of the same information.
Inconsistent data
Versions of the same fact that disagree.
Centralised data
Data whose main records are maintained in a common shared store.
Synchronisation
Keeping copies in step by exchanging updates.
Shared data
Information made available to relevant authorised systems and users.
Migration
Moving existing records into a new system.
Trade-off
A gain in one area alongside a cost or difficulty in another.
Implementation
The process of putting a system into use successfully.
Integration
Making separate systems work together.
Access control
Rules about who can reach or change data.
Productivity
How effectively users can complete their work.

Remember

Weigh both sides

  • Shared systems can improve access and productivity.
  • They can also increase cost, complexity, and security risk.
  • Strong answers balance benefits against concerns.
